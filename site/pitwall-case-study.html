Work/Case study

Pitwall: comparing F1 teams across 68 seasons.

Every Formula 1 constructors' championship from 1958 to the latest race weekend, in one dashboard. A data pipeline checks and cleans seventy years of race results, and the dashboard compares teams by their share of each season's points, because a win has been worth anywhere from 8 to 25.

In 30 seconds

What
An archive and dashboard of every constructors' championship since the first in 1958: 1,076 Grands Prix, 12,700+ team race results and 175 teams.
How
A Python pipeline in bronze, silver and gold stages (Pandas, Parquet, DuckDB SQL) joins, checks and aggregates the raw results. Teams are compared by their share of each season's points, so different scoring eras line up.
Result
Ferrari in 2002 and McLaren in 1988 each took half of all points, the most dominant seasons on record. Mercedes' 8 straight titles were the longest run, but at 33% of the points, not the most dominant era.

01Problem

A win isn't worth what it used to be.

Why I built this

I'm a Formula 1 fan, and I love learning the history of almost anything. F1 has 75 years of it, recorded race by race, so I wanted to build something that let me explore it: which teams really ruled their era, and how the sport changed around them.

Red Bull scored 860 points in 2023. McLaren scored 199 in 1988. On raw points, Red Bull's season looks four times as strong, yet both teams won all but one race.

The rules moved under the numbers. A win was worth 8 points in 1958 and is worth 25 today. Seasons grew from 11 races to 24. Points that once went only to the top five or six now reach tenth, and sprint races add more. A season total from one era means little next to one from another.

  • Raw points make every modern team look stronger than every classic one.
  • Titles alone hide whether a champion cruised or scraped through.

So the question: which teams really dominated their era, and can a dashboard make that comparison fair at a glance?

02Approach

Clean it once, then compare by share.

A pipeline in stages

The raw data is 14 CSV tables of historical results, about 720,000 rows. I built the pipeline as bronze, silver and gold stages, each saved as Parquet and each checking its own output before the next stage reads it:

  1. 1Bronze: join each team's race result to its date and name; every race–team pair must be unique
  2. 2Silver: fix types, remove duplicate rows, blank out impossible values and log every issue
  3. 3Gold: DuckDB SQL totals each team's points by month and the change from the month before
  4. 4Export: one CSV for the first dashboard, rebuilt with a single command
LAG(points_m) OVER (PARTITION BY constructor_id ORDER BY m) AS prev_points_m

A window function finds each team's previous month in one pass. Where there is no previous month, the change is left empty rather than shown as a false 0%.

Points share puts eras side by side

A team's points share is its percentage of all the points scored that season. McLaren's 199 points in 1988 were 49.8% of the 400 handed out; Red Bull's 860 in 2023 were 35.0% of 2,458. The scoring system cancels out, and the two seasons become comparable.

Rules that keep the numbers honest

Official totals
Standings, not sumsSeason points come from the official standings, so penalties count.
Exclusions
Counted as zeroMcLaren's 2007 exclusion shows as EX and scores nothing.
Sprints
Points, not winsSprint points count toward the title; sprint wins don't count as Grand Prix wins.
Partial seasons
Kept apart2025 is in the archive through August but never counts as a title.

What I took away

  • Longest isn't strongest. Mercedes' eight titles are the record, but McLaren in 1988–91 and Ferrari in 1999–2004 took a bigger share of the points while they won.
  • Share narrows the gap between eras, but doesn't close it. Red Bull won 21 of 22 races in 2023 yet took 35% of the points, against McLaren's 49.8% for 15 of 16 in 1988. With points now reaching tenth place, rivals collect more of them.
  • Data rules change the story. Ferrari's 45.8% in 2007 is high because McLaren, excluded that year, counts as zero. With McLaren's 218 points counted, Ferrari's share would have been 30.8%.
  • The closest fight is recent. McLaren beat Ferrari by 14 points in 2024, 25.0% of the points against 24.5%: the smallest gap at the top since 1958.

Seasons 1958–2024 are complete; 2025 runs through August and is left out. In 2024, McLaren scored 666 and Ferrari 652 of the 2,659 points.

04Engineering

A pipeline first, then an app on top.

How it fits together

My pipeline: from raw results to the first dashboard

  1. Raw tables14 CSV tables of results, standings, races and teams, 1950 to 2025
  2. Bronze and silverPandas joins, checks and cleans them into Parquet
  3. GoldDuckDB SQL: monthly points and change per team
  4. Monthly dashboardChart.js and Bootstrap, still online as the monthly explorer

The Pitwall app layer, contributed by a collaborator

  1. ImporterPython: checks keys and counts, then loads in one transaction
  2. PostgreSQLTeams, seasons, races, results and standings in their own schema
  3. Express APIValidated filters, clear errors and CSV export
  4. PitwallOverview, profiles, comparisons and insights, on GitHub Pages
Checked at every stage
Fails fastThe stages assert their columns, types and unique keys, so bad data stops the run instead of reaching a chart.
Repeatable
One commandpython -m src.tracker.pipeline rebuilds everything from the raw tables. After a cleanup, the rebuilt CSV was byte-identical.
Columnar
Parquet and DuckDBEach stage hands Parquet to the next, and DuckDB runs SQL straight on the files, no database server needed.
Also into PostgreSQL
FastF1 loaderAn optional loader I wrote pulls race results from 2018 on with FastF1 and writes them to PostgreSQL with SQLAlchemy.
Same numbers everywhere
One statistics moduleIn the app layer, the browser and the API share one module, so a total is identical on screen, in the API and in an export.
Runs without a server
Static demoThe live app falls back to a bundled snapshot of the data, so it runs on GitHub Pages with no backend.

06What's next

What I'd build next.

Built since the first version

  • One scoring system for every era. A switch re-scores every race with today's points, so eras compare like with like. McLaren's 1988 season goes from 199 to 576 points, and the 1958 title moves from Vanwall to Ferrari, because back then only each team's best car scored.
  • Automatic updates. After every race weekend, a scheduled job loads the new results from the Jolpica F1 API, rebuilds the data, runs the tests and publishes the update. This site copies it the same day.

Still on the list

  1. A hosted API. The live demo runs on a snapshot. Deploying the Express API with PostgreSQL would put the REST endpoints online too.
  2. One pipeline. My staged pipeline and the app's importer read the same raw tables separately. Feeding both from one pipeline would remove the duplication.
